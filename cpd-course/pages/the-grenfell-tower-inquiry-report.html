About the Grenfell Tower Inquiry

The Grenfell Tower Inquiry was established in September 2017 by the UK government following the Grenfell Tower fire. Chaired by Sir Martin Moore-Bick, the Inquiry examined the causes of the fire, the building’s refurbishment, and the response of emergency services and public authorities. It involved survivors, bereaved families, local residents, government bodies, industry experts, and construction and manufacturing companies.

Major incident escalation

  1. 01:26

    MPS declares a Major Incident.

  2. 01:27

    Fire reaches the roof and begins horizontal spread.

  3. 01:29

    Incident Commander (IC) Michael Dowden requests “pumps 20”.

  4. 01:31

    By this time, 110 out of 297 occupants have escaped.

  5. 01:42

    LAS declares a Significant Incident; the police helicopter (NPAS) arrives at 01:45.

02:04–02:44

Extensive spread

Fire spreads around the crown and diagonally across the building, affecting south-east and north-west corners. Command handovers continue between SM Walton, GM Richard Welch, and DAC Andrew O’Loughlin.

  1. 02:06

    GM Welch declares a Major Incident.

  2. 02:26

    LAS declares a Major Incident.

02:35–02:47

Revocation of “stay put”

The critical turning point in the disaster response:

  1. 02:35

    Control room decides to revoke “stay put” advice and tells callers to leave.

  2. 02:44–02:47

    Andrew Roe takes command at 02:44 and revokes ‘stay put’ advice at 02:47.

02:50–08:07

Final evacuations

  1. 02:50

    Commissioner Dany Cotton arrives at the scene.

  2. 03:20

    First Tactical Co-ordination Group (TCG) meeting held.

  3. 04:02

    Fires on south and west elevations converge at the top corner.

  4. 08:07

    Elpidio Bonifacio, the last survivor to leave the tower, is evacuated.

Reflect on advice and responsibility

Pause and reflect

The ‘stay put’ strategy relied on fires being contained within individual flats. At Grenfell, this protection had failed: the fire reached the roof at 01:27, but the control room did not decide to advise callers to leave until 02:35, and the incident commander formally revoked ‘stay put’ at 02:47. Residents faced life-threatening conditions, often with limited information and options. As an educator, how would you help learners examine why official advice remained in place despite mounting evidence of its failure? What questions does this raise about institutional responsibility, accountability and trust?

A teaching perspective

Learners could examine the timeline alongside residents’ testimonies, emergency calls and findings from the Grenfell Tower Inquiry. They could consider why ‘stay put’ was ordinarily considered appropriate in buildings designed to contain fires within individual flats, and how the rapid spread of fire at Grenfell undermined this assumption.

The discussion should avoid judging residents for following or departing from official advice, recognising that they had different levels of information and opportunities to escape. Instead, attention could turn to how institutions recognise emerging evidence, question established procedures and respond when circumstances change.

This case raises important questions about the relationship between expertise, authority and public trust. Trust should not require unquestioning obedience, but should be supported by institutional competence, transparency, responsiveness to evidence and accountability. Educators could encourage learners to consider how institutions can earn, maintain and rebuild trust when their decisions have serious consequences.
